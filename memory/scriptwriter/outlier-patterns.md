# Outlier patterns

Distilled from 5 top-performing videos across three reference channels (Crayon Capital, Billionaire Bears, The Wealth Explainer — combined ~16M views on the analyzed videos alone). The patterns below are *structural and tonal*, not topical. Use them as a menu the script can pull from, not as a rigid template — the 5-beat template in `episode-template.md` still owns the macro arc.

---

## Cold-open: pick ONE of three hook forms

Never open with a thesis sentence ("In this video we'll look at…"). Pick one:

### A. Contradiction stack
Three short factual clauses that conflict. Numbers preferred over adjectives.

> *"He lied about a $1 stock, made millions, and only served 22 months. Then Hollywood turned his crimes into a blockbuster."* — Crayon Capital, Wolf of Wall Street

### B. Second-person "you-are-here"
Drop the viewer inside the moment. Highest-outlier device across the dataset.

> *"You woke up rich. You went to bed broke. No war, no warning, just a terrifying crash that wiped out $200 billion."* — Crayon Capital, Great Depression

### C. Date + place + character
Start with a grounded coordinate; let the character carry the rest.

> *"In 2005, billionaire investor Masayoshi was looking for a new way to grow his wealth."* — Billionaire Bears, BNF

---

## Earn-the-watch: moral or dramatic, not informational

The first 90 seconds need a stakes line — not a thesis. Land it *before* the "who profits?" brand line, not instead of.

Working examples (verbatim from outliers):
- *"And the wildest part — people still cheer for him."*
- *"Fix him or join him."*
- *"That's the first mistake."*
- *"You'll regret this for the rest of your life."*

Then immediately pivot to the brand line:
> [Stakes line — moral contradiction or implicit accusation.] So let's ask the only question that actually matters: who profits?

## Transition phrase library

The outliers almost never use "next…", "meanwhile…", or "additionally…". Use these instead:

- **"But here's the magic. [Mechanism]."** — hinge from observation to mechanism
- **"Enter [Name/Force]."** — compresses a full introduction into two words
- **"[Year]. [Place]."** — comma-splice scene change, no verb. e.g. *"October 29th, 1929. New York."*
- **"[Actor X] called it [polite name]. [Actor Y] called it [real name]."** — the highest-density framing device in the dataset. e.g. *"Jordan called it asset protection. The FBI called it laundering."*
- **"And just like that, [next phase]."**
- **"Then came the [thing]."** / **"And then came the [thing]."**
- **"But [Subject] didn't. Instead, [counter-action]."**

Banned: "Next…", "Furthermore…", "Additionally…", "Moreover…", "In conclusion…", "Let's move on to…".

---

## Three-item list, third subverts

Used in every outlier. Format: `[concrete A], [concrete B], [concrete C that breaks the pattern]`. Carries the dry-skeptical tone without editorializing.

> *"Strippers in the conference room, champagne overflowing into fax machines, lines of coke snorted off compliance manuals."*

> *"Cup ramen, anime, and spending most of his days alone in a room day trading."*

> *"You die in a hospital bed, a prison cell, or on the pavement outside a steakhouse."*

Rule: the third item should reframe the first two — either by escalation (rhino-killing narcotics), incongruity (anime), or grim verdict (pavement).

---

## Sign-off: one sentence, under 15 words, verdict not summary

Every outlier ends on a single short verdict that reframes everything previous. No CTA. No "thanks for watching." No "subscribe."

Working models:
- *"We wanted to be him."* (5 words)
- *"This is the life you chose."* (7 words)
- *"Now you've become one."* (4 words)
- *"This time the whole world would feel it."* (8 words — also a cliffhanger to next episode)

Banned outros: "thanks for watching", "subscribe", "let me know in the comments", "and that's the story of…", any rhetorical question.

---

## Channel-specific tone notes

- **Crayon Capital (Wolf of Wall Street, Great Depression):** wry observer with moral undertone. Lets juxtaposed facts ("$110M restitution / only a fraction paid") do the moralizing. Closest to prizm's target voice — adopt their rhythm and number-density. Reject the wisecracks (Great Depression's "go touch grass" lands for their audience but conflicts with our investigator persona).
- **Billionaire Bears (BNF):** mythologizing and reverent, slow contemplative pacing, heavy music. Useful for episodes built around a single legendary figure; not the default mode.
- **The Wealth Explainer (Black Market Surgeon, Italian Mafia):** second-person noir, almost no narrator voice. Coldest tone in the set; highest single outlier. The metaphor-as-thesis trick ("You spent your whole life saving animals. Now you've become one.") is gold.

## Two experimental templates worth one episode each

These break the 5-beat template deliberately. Don't default to them; reach for them when the topic invites.

### Pure-list mode
Twelve identically-shaped beats, each ~45 seconds, each with the same micro-template: (a) "Level N, the X." (b) "You are…" (c) duties. (d) risk/promotion mechanism. The structure *is* the entertainment.

Candidate prizm topics: "Every rung of the private-equity ladder", "Every player in a bank run", "Every level of a tax-loss harvest."

### Pure second-person noir
The narrator disappears. The viewer *is* the character for the full runtime. Sacrifices the investigator voice but dramatizes the mechanism instead of explaining it.

Candidate prizm topic: "You are a Treasury bond trader on Friday, March 12, 2023" — first-person account of a bank failure morning.

Flag both with the user before adopting on a real episode.
