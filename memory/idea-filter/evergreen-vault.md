## 1. War / military escalation → War-economy profits

Wars are paid for two ways: taxation (slow, unpopular) and debt monetization (fast, invisible). Every modern war is a transfer from future taxpayers and bondholders to today's defense contractors and energy producers. The mechanism: government issues bonds, central bank ends up holding many of them, money supply expands, defense stocks rally on guaranteed multi-year orders. Lockheed Martin, Raytheon, Northrop Grumman, General Dynamics, BAE — these five capture the majority of US contract dollars. The 2001–2021 "war on terror" added roughly $8 trillion to US debt; defense-sector market cap roughly tripled over the same window.

Historical parallels: the British East India Company financed by the Crown through the Napoleonic wars; J.P. Morgan's underwriting of Allied munitions in WWI (which dragged the US into the war via creditor self-interest); the post-WWII "military-industrial complex" Eisenhower warned about; Halliburton's no-bid Iraq contracts under Cheney.

Common misconception: that war is bad for the economy. Aggregate GDP often rises during war; the question is who owns the gains. Answer: bondholders, contractors, energy.

## 2. Tech layoffs → How layoffs pump stock prices

Layoffs are a coordination signal more than a cost-cutting measure. When one major tech company announces layoffs, peers follow within weeks — not because they all suddenly need to cut, but because the market rewards "discipline." Meta's 2022 layoffs preceded a stock more-than-doubling in 2023; the "Year of Efficiency" framing was a deliberate narrative product. The mechanism: layoffs reduce forward expenses, signal management discipline to institutional shareholders, and trigger upward EPS revisions. Cost savings often go straight into buybacks, which mechanically lift EPS further by shrinking share count.

Historical parallels: the "Chainsaw Al" Dunlap era at Scott Paper and Sunbeam in the 1990s; GE under Jack Welch in the 1980s ("rank and yank" 10% annual cuts); IBM's 1993 cuts under Gerstner.

Named beneficiary candidates: Mark Zuckerberg (Meta), Andy Jassy (Amazon), Sundar Pichai (Google). The mechanism is identical across all three.

## 3. AI breakthrough or bubble talk → Four stages of every bubble

Every asset bubble follows the same four-stage pattern, first formalized by Charles Kindleberger and Hyman Minsky:
1. **Displacement** — a real innovation creates genuine new value (railroads, internet, AI).
2. **Boom** — credit expands to chase the innovation. Prices rise faster than fundamentals.
3. **Euphoria** — retail and tourist investors arrive. New metrics are invented to justify prices ("eyeballs", "tokens", "compute").
4. **Crash** — a marginal seller can't find a buyer. Liquidity vanishes. Prices reset to fundamentals.

Historical parallels: Dutch tulips (1637), South Sea Bubble (1720), British railway mania (1840s), Florida land boom (1920s), Japanese real estate (1989), dotcom (2000), US housing (2008), crypto (2018, 2022).

The lesson is not "AI is a bubble." The lesson is the pattern — and that the innovation always *is* real. Railroads did change the world; most railroad investors still lost their shirts.

## 4. Celebrity / athlete bankruptcy → Lifestyle-inflation math

The Sports Illustrated estimate: roughly 78% of NFL players are bankrupt or under financial stress within two years of retirement; the NBA number is around 60% within five years. The mechanism is not stupidity — it is **lifestyle inflation against a short earnings window**. A 25-year-old earning $5M/yr buys a $5M house. The carrying cost (taxes, insurance, maintenance, staff) is roughly 5% per year = $250K/yr forever. When the contract ends at 30, the carrying cost remains. Multiply by car, jewelry, entourage, child support.

The math: a $50M career, taxed at 40% federal + state + agent fees ≈ $25M take-home. Sustained at $1.5M/yr cost of living = ~17 years of runway with zero growth, less if invested poorly. Anything more aggressive than that profile compounds toward bankruptcy.

Historical parallels: Mike Tyson ($300M earned, bankrupt 2003), Allen Iverson ($154M, bankrupt 2012), Curt Schilling ($114M, 38 Studios collapse), Nicolas Cage's tax problems, MC Hammer.

## 5. Natural disaster → Disaster capitalism

The term is Naomi Klein's. The mechanism: catastrophic events suspend normal political resistance to policies that would otherwise be unpopular. In the resulting vacuum, well-positioned firms capture infrastructure contracts, real estate, and regulatory concessions at far below normal cost. Insurance companies refuse coverage in the affected zone; private equity buys distressed property; rebuilding contracts go to firms with prior relationships in the capital.

Historical parallels: New Orleans post-Katrina (school system privatization, public housing demolition); Puerto Rico post-Maria (electricity grid privatization, Whitefish Energy); Haiti post-2010 earthquake (Clinton Foundation contracts, garment-industry tax breaks); Iraq post-invasion (Bechtel, Halliburton).

Named beneficiaries: Bechtel, KBR, Whitefish Energy, Blackstone's post-2008 housing roll-up.

## 6. Election cycle → Presidential market cycle

The pattern, robust since 1928: the S&P 500 typically performs worst in years 1–2 of a presidential term (the "pain years" when bad news is timed) and best in years 3–4 (the "incumbent re-election" years when stimulus, rate cuts, and good news are timed). The mechanism is incumbent self-interest plus monetary policy timing — recessions are easier to absorb early in a term.

Average year-by-year returns since 1928: Year 1 ~7%, Year 2 ~4%, Year 3 ~14%, Year 4 ~8%. Year 3 is the standout.

Historical parallels: Reagan 1983–84 recovery; Clinton 1995–96; Obama 2011–12; Trump 2019; Biden 2023.

The episode angle: this is not a partisan pattern. It works regardless of party. It works because both parties want to be re-elected.

## 7. Crypto crash or pump → Greater fool theory

Greater fool: an asset has value only if you can sell it to someone willing to pay more. The mechanism does not require the asset to produce cash flow or have intrinsic utility. It requires only a continuous supply of buyers with higher conviction or higher risk tolerance than the seller. When the supply of fools runs out, the price falls to its fundamental floor — which for non-cash-flowing assets can be near zero.

Bitcoin's defenders argue scarcity-as-utility; this is a separate argument. The relevant point for the bucket is: when an asset is purchased with the primary thesis being "it will go up", that is greater fool, regardless of asset class. Beanie Babies, NFTs, GameStop, dotcom stocks with no revenue, Florida swampland in 1925 — same mechanism.

Historical parallels: tulip bulbs (1637), Beanie Babies (1996), pets.com (2000), Florida land (1925), NFTs (2021), FTX users (2022).

## 8. Sovereign default → How countries actually go bankrupt

Countries don't run out of money. They run out of *trust*. Default happens when bondholders refuse to roll over debt at sustainable rates. The mechanism unfolds in stages: rising deficit → debt-to-GDP climbs → bond yields rise to compensate for risk → debt service eats more of the budget → austerity or money-printing → currency falls → import inflation → political crisis → restructuring or hyperinflation.

A country that borrows in its own currency (USA, Japan, UK) defaults via inflation. A country that borrows in foreign currency (Argentina, Greece, Sri Lanka, Zambia) defaults via missed payment.

Historical parallels: Argentina (eight defaults since independence, most recently 2020); Greece 2012; Russia 1998; Mexico 1982; Weimar Germany 1923; Zimbabwe 2008; Sri Lanka 2022; Lebanon 2020.

## 9. Major fraud / heist headline → How money laundering really works

Three stages, taught in every AML course:
1. **Placement** — get cash into the financial system. Hardest stage. Done via cash-intensive businesses (car washes, restaurants, casinos), structured deposits, or cross-border smuggling.
2. **Layering** — disguise the trail. Move funds through shell companies in multiple jurisdictions (Delaware, BVI, Cyprus, Singapore). Use trade-based laundering (over- or under-invoicing imports). Buy and sell artwork, real estate, or sports clubs.
3. **Integration** — bring "clean" money back into the legitimate economy. Buy real estate, businesses, luxury goods.

Mechanism: every major bank has been fined for facilitating this. HSBC ($1.9B in 2012 for Mexican cartel money), Danske Bank ($2B+ Estonia scandal), Deutsche Bank multiple times, Wells Fargo, JPMorgan (Epstein-related), BNP Paribas, ING.

Historical parallels: Meyer Lansky in 1950s Cuba; BCCI in the 1980s; Pablo Escobar's Medellín cartel; the Russian "Laundromat" via Moldovan courts (2010–2014).

## 10. Inflation print → Hidden wealth transfer

Inflation is not a tax everyone pays equally. It is a transfer from holders of cash and fixed-rate creditors to holders of real assets and fixed-rate debtors. If you own a house with a 3% mortgage and inflation runs 6%, your real interest rate is negative — the bank is paying you to hold the house. If you hold $100K in a checking account, inflation has taken $6K of purchasing power.

Asset owners (top 10% of US households hold ~70% of stocks, top 1% hold ~50% of stocks; homeowners are concentrated in upper income brackets) are net beneficiaries. Renters and cash savers are net losers.

Historical parallels: 1970s US (best decade for gold and real estate, worst for bonds); Weimar Germany (industrialists with hard assets emerged richer); post-pandemic 2021–2023 (US household wealth gap widened sharply); Argentina, ongoing.

## 11. Housing market move → 18-year Harrison housing cycle

Economist Fred Harrison and others have documented an ~18-year cycle in US and UK property: roughly 14 years of expansion, 4 years of contraction. The mechanism is the land value tax dynamic — productivity gains capitalize into land prices, which encourage speculation, which crowds out productive investment, which causes a correction.

Recorded peaks: 1818, 1836, 1854, 1872, 1890, 1907, 1925, *anomaly window during WWII*, 1973, 1990, 2007, projected ~2026.

The episode angle: housing crashes are not random and not caused by the most-recent headline. They are scheduled.

## 12. Strike / wage news → Real wages over 100 years

US real (inflation-adjusted) hourly wages for production workers peaked around 1973 and have been roughly flat in real terms ever since, while productivity per worker has continued rising. The gap between productivity and wages — once closely tracked through 1973 — is the mechanism by which the surplus has flowed to capital instead of labor.

The episode angle: every individual strike is a small data point in a 50-year structural story. The 2023 UAW strike, Hollywood writers' strike, port worker strikes — same underlying mechanism.

Historical parallels: 1936 Flint sit-down strike; 1981 PATCO air traffic controllers (Reagan firing 11,000); 1997 UPS Teamsters; 2018–2019 teacher walkouts; 2023 SAG-AFTRA / WGA.

## 13. Tax law change → How the wealthy actually pay less

Three primary mechanisms:
1. **Income shifted to capital gains** — taxed at ~20% federal vs ~37% top ordinary rate. Achieved via stock-based comp, founder shares, carried interest.
2. **Depreciation against active income** — real estate depreciation deductions can offset rental and sometimes ordinary income. Cost segregation accelerates this. This is why every billionaire owns real estate.
3. **Step-up in basis at death** — heirs inherit assets at fair market value, eliminating all unrealized capital gains. Combined with buy-borrow-die, this allows multi-generational wealth to compound essentially tax-free.

Episode angle: it's not that the wealthy evade taxes. They use a code written for them.

Named beneficiaries: any name on the Forbes 400. ProPublica's 2021 IRS leak documented Bezos, Musk, Buffett, Bloomberg paying single-digit effective tax rates.

## 14. Famous IPO → Pre-IPO winners

Mechanism: by the time a company IPOs, the early investors have already made their money. Pre-IPO valuation gains are captured by founders, employees with stock options, VCs (Sequoia, A16z, Benchmark), and late-stage private investors (Tiger Global, Coatue, SoftBank). The IPO is the *exit* for these holders. Retail buyers are providing the liquidity for the early holders to sell.

Lockup periods (typically 180 days) explain post-IPO price patterns: a sharp drop is common around lockup expiration as insiders sell. Direct listings (Spotify, Coinbase) skip the lockup mechanic.

Historical parallels: Facebook IPO 2012 (retail bought at $38, dropped to $17; insiders sold the top); WeWork's *failed* IPO 2019 (saved retail from a disaster); Snap 2017; Lyft 2019; Rivian 2021.

## 15. Bank failure → Fractional reserve banking

Banks lend out most of the deposits they hold. A bank with $100M in deposits might hold only $10M in cash and reserves. As long as withdrawals are random, this works. When withdrawals correlate (a run), it doesn't — there is no scenario where every depositor can be paid back at once. The FDIC was created in 1933 to make runs irrational; deposit insurance up to $250K means a small depositor has no reason to flee.

The mechanism that breaks this: large uninsured deposits. SVB 2023 — over 90% of deposits were above the FDIC limit, concentrated among VC-backed startups who all banked there and all watched each other's Twitter accounts. Coordination became trivial.

Historical parallels: 1907 Panic (J.P. Morgan as private central bank); 1933 wave of failures (Roosevelt's bank holiday); Continental Illinois 1984; Northern Rock 2007 (UK, first run in 150 years); Bear Stearns 2008; SVB / First Republic / Signature 2023.

## 16. Olympics / World Cup awarded → Why hosting bankrupts cities

Almost every modern Olympics has run over its original budget by 100–300%. The mechanism: the bid is made when the host city is most optimistic. Construction commitments are sunk cost before reality intervenes. Venues built for two weeks become permanent maintenance liabilities. Tourism never recovers the projected returns because (a) regular tourists are deterred by Olympic crowds and (b) infrastructure designed for peak load is mostly empty afterward.

Documented cost overruns: Montreal 1976 (took 30 years to pay off the debt); Athens 2004 (often cited as contributing to Greek debt crisis); Sochi 2014 (~$51B, most expensive ever); Rio 2016 (state went bankrupt months before opening); Tokyo 2020 (~3× original budget).

Counterexamples: LA 1984 ran a surplus by reusing existing infrastructure. The lesson: hosting works only if you skip the build-out.

## 17. New Forbes billionaire → How fortunes are actually built

Three patterns capture most modern fortunes:
1. **Concentrated equity in one company you didn't sell.** Bezos, Musk, Zuckerberg, Buffett, Gates — all held >10% of one company for decades.
2. **Inheritance and family business compounding.** Walton, Mars, Koch, Hermès, Ambani.
3. **Leverage on appreciating assets.** Real estate dynasties (Trump, Ross), private equity (Schwarzman, Black).

Almost none come from "diversified investing" or "passive index funds." Index funds preserve wealth; concentration creates it. The episode angle: the financial-advice industry promotes a path that is statistically optimal for preserving small fortunes but virtually never produces large ones.

Named entry points: a new Forbes billionaire announcement is an opportunity to trace which of the three patterns applies.

## 18. Pharma scandal → How drug pricing really works

US drug prices are set by what insurers and PBMs (pharmacy benefit managers) will pay, not by manufacturing cost. The same drug costs 3–10× more in the US than in Europe because European national health systems negotiate as monopsonies; the US fragments into thousands of insurers, none with enough leverage individually. Three PBMs (CVS Caremark, Express Scripts, OptumRx) handle ~80% of US prescriptions and extract rebates from manufacturers that don't necessarily flow to patients.

Patent extension games: Humira's roughly 130 patent applications extended exclusivity by years past the original molecule patent. Insulin pricing rose >700% from 2000 to 2020 with no significant innovation in the molecule.

Named beneficiaries: Pfizer, AbbVie, Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk, the three PBMs, hospital systems.

## 19. Oil price shock → Petrodollar system

In 1974, the US struck an agreement with Saudi Arabia: oil would be priced exclusively in dollars in exchange for US security guarantees. Every country that wanted to buy oil needed dollars. This created permanent global demand for the dollar, allowing the US to run deficits other countries could not. Oil price isn't just oil — it's a dollar-cycle event.

The mechanism: oil price up → petroleum-producing countries accumulate dollars → those dollars get recycled into US Treasuries and US assets → US asset prices supported → US can finance deficits.

The mechanism breaks when major buyers transact outside the dollar. The 2010s–2020s saw early steps: yuan-denominated oil contracts in Shanghai, Russia–India trade in rupees, BRICS settlement talks.

Historical parallels: 1973 OPEC embargo (the original shock); 1979 Iranian Revolution; 1990 Gulf War; 2014 collapse (Saudi-engineered to hurt US shale); 2022 sanctions on Russian oil.

## 20. Supply chain / port news → Just-in-time inventory and chokepoint economics

Post-Toyota (1970s), Western manufacturing optimized for inventory turns. Holding inventory was waste; everything arrived "just in time." This raised aggregate profit margins by reducing working capital — and made the system catastrophically fragile to any disruption.

Five global chokepoints matter disproportionately: Strait of Hormuz (~20% of world oil), Strait of Malacca (~25% of global trade), Suez Canal (~12% of global trade), Panama Canal (~5%), Bab el-Mandeb (Red Sea, ~10%). A single ship grounding in Suez (Ever Given, 2021) added an estimated $400M/hr to global trade costs.

Episode angle: every supply-chain news story is a story about fragility that was deliberately engineered for margin.

Historical parallels: 1956 Suez crisis; 2011 Tōhoku earthquake (auto industry); 2020–21 semiconductor shortage; Houthi Red Sea attacks 2024.
