# Video concepts — 2026-05-15

## Idea 1 — Who really profits when Hormuz closes?

- **News trigger:** Strait of Hormuz shutdown has pushed Brent toward $110, with 10–11M bpd removed from accessible supply — the largest oil shock by volume in modern history (01-news.md candidate 5).
- **Evergreen mechanism:** The petrodollar loop. Since 1974, oil priced in dollars has forced every importer to first acquire dollars, recycling those dollars into Treasuries and US assets. Every spike in oil isn't just an energy story — it's a refill of the dollar engine that lets the US run deficits no other country can. The episode shows the pipe: Riyadh sells barrels for dollars, parks dollars in Treasuries, US borrows cheaply, US consumer pays at the pump. The mechanism stays true whether oil is $40 or $200; only the volume of the recycle changes.
- **Three historical parallels:**
  1. 1973 — OPEC embargo — quadrupled crude, birthed the original petrodollar deal a year later.
  2. 1979 — Iranian Revolution — second oil shock; Volcker raised rates to defend the dollar, not oil.
  3. 2014 — Saudi-engineered crude collapse — designed to bankrupt US shale and discipline Russia.
- **Central villain or beneficiary:** Saudi Aramco.
- **Freshness window:** 3–6 weeks while Brent stays elevated.
- **Why this passes the test:** Hot war headline plus a 50-year monetary mechanism that explains why every American gas-station price tag is a Treasury auction in disguise.

## Idea 2 — How Meta's 8,000 layoffs became a buy signal

- **News trigger:** Meta is firing 8,000 employees on May 20 while spending $135B on AI infrastructure (01-news.md candidate 2).
- **Evergreen mechanism:** Layoffs are a coordination signal, not a cost cut. One headline tech firm announces, peers follow within weeks, and institutional shareholders reward the "discipline" with multiple expansion. The dollars saved rarely fund anything new — they fund buybacks, which shrink share count and mechanically lift EPS. Meta's 2023 "Year of Efficiency" was the template: stock more than doubled while 21,000 workers were cut. The episode shows the exact pipe from termination email to upward EPS revision to a CEO with a larger stock-comp package.
- **Three historical parallels:**
  1. 1993 — IBM under Gerstner — 60,000 cut, stock tripled over the decade.
  2. 1996 — "Chainsaw Al" Dunlap at Sunbeam — proved the market loves the announcement more than the result.
  3. 2023 — Meta's "Year of Efficiency" — stock doubled while Reality Labs kept losing $16B/yr.
- **Central villain or beneficiary:** Mark Zuckerberg.
- **Freshness window:** 4–6 weeks; reinforced by every Q2 earnings call that follows.
- **Why this passes the test:** A named CEO, a dated firing, and a mechanism that has worked identically since Welch ran GE.

## Idea 3 — The Cerebras IPO was already over before it opened

- **News trigger:** Cerebras opened up nearly 70% in the biggest Nasdaq debut of 2026, raising $5.55B at $185 and printing $350 on day one (01-news.md candidate 1).
- **Evergreen mechanism:** By the time a hot IPO rings the bell, the money has already been made — and not by the people buying that morning. Founders, employees, Sequoia, Benchmark, Tiger, and SoftBank locked in their gains across the private rounds. The IPO is their exit, and retail provides the liquidity. The 180-day lockup explains the recurring six-month-post-IPO drop: insiders sell into whatever price retail has manufactured. The episode walks through Cerebras's cap table — who paid $2/share in 2017, who bought at $185, and who has to sell by November.
- **Three historical parallels:**
  1. 2012 — Facebook IPO — retail bought $38, crashed to $17 while insiders cashed out.
  2. 2019 — WeWork — the IPO that *failed* and accidentally saved retail $40B.
  3. 2021 — Rivian — opened at $129, sits below $15 today after lockup unwind.
- **Central villain or beneficiary:** Benchmark Capital (lead Cerebras Series A, ~$2/share basis).
- **Freshness window:** 2–3 weeks while the IPO is still trending; revisits cleanly at the November lockup expiration.
- **Why this passes the test:** A specific named IPO, a specific named VC, and a mechanism that has stripped retail at every hot debut since Netscape 1995.

## Idea 4 — The 3.8% CPI print is a wealth transfer in disguise

- **News trigger:** April CPI hit 3.8% YoY, the highest reading since May 2023, with energy driving over 40% of the gain (01-news.md candidate 4).
- **Evergreen mechanism:** Inflation isn't a tax everyone pays equally — it's a directional transfer. If you hold a 3% mortgage while CPI runs 4%, the bank is paying you to own the house. If you hold $100K in checking, you just lost $4K of purchasing power to no one in particular — except the borrower on the other side of that cash. The top 10% of households hold roughly 70% of US stocks and most of the leveraged real estate; the bottom half holds cash and rents. Every CPI print is a quiet rebalancing from the second group to the first. The episode shows the math household by household.
- **Three historical parallels:**
  1. 1970s — gold and housing crushed bonds and cash; the decade quietly minted the boomer asset class.
  2. 1923 — Weimar Germany — industrialists with hard assets emerged richer, savers were wiped.
  3. 2021–2023 — the largest single-cycle US wealth-gap widening on record.
- **Central villain or beneficiary:** The top 10% of US households (asset-holders).
- **Freshness window:** 3 weeks until the May CPI print on June 11.
- **Why this passes the test:** A monthly headline everyone half-skims, reframed as a transfer with named winners and losers.

## Idea 5 — The $16B Banco Master fraud washes through three countries

- **News trigger:** Brazilian federal police arrested Daniel Vorcaro's father as the $16B Banco Master scandal pulled in Flávio Bolsonaro and a $325M Tether loan (01-news.md candidate 8).
- **Evergreen mechanism:** Three stages, taught in every AML course. Placement: get the cash into the system through a friendly bank or a stablecoin issuer that doesn't ask. Layering: shell companies in BVI, Delaware, Cyprus; trade-based invoicing; loans against opaque collateral. Integration: real estate, sports clubs, art, businesses. Every major bank has been fined for facilitating it — HSBC paid $1.9B for the Sinaloa cartel; Danske Bank moved $230B through one Estonian branch. The episode uses Banco Master as a live diagram of the three stages, with Tether as the new placement vector that didn't exist in 2010.
- **Three historical parallels:**
  1. 1980s — BCCI — laundered for arms dealers, CIA, Noriega; collapsed at $20B.
  2. 2012 — HSBC fined $1.9B for Mexican cartel laundering; nobody went to prison.
  3. 2018 — Danske Bank Estonia — $230B in suspicious flows through a single branch.
- **Central villain or beneficiary:** Tether Holdings.
- **Freshness window:** 4–8 weeks as Brazilian indictments unfold.
- **Why this passes the test:** A live, named, multi-country fraud is the perfect doorway into the placement-layering-integration framework.

## Idea 6 — The 2026 housing crack was on the calendar

- **News trigger:** 30-year mortgage rates jumped to a 5-week high of 6.54% as Iran-war oil and hot CPI pushed yields up (01-news.md candidate 12).
- **Evergreen mechanism:** The Harrison 18-year property cycle — roughly 14 years of expansion, 4 years of contraction. Recorded US peaks: 1818, 1836, 1854, 1872, 1890, 1907, 1925, 1973, 1990, 2007, projected ~2026. The mechanism is land-value capitalization: productivity gains get absorbed into land prices, which encourage speculation, which crowds out productive investment, which forces correction. The episode shows that this rate spike isn't the cause of anything — it's the trigger event a 200-year-old cycle has been pointing at since the last peak in 2007.
- **Three historical parallels:**
  1. 1925 — Florida land boom — collapsed two years before the 1929 stock crash.
  2. 1990 — Japan property peak — followed by 30 years of deflation.
  3. 2007 — US housing peak — exactly 18 years before today.
- **Central villain or beneficiary:** Fred Harrison (the beneficiary here is the analyst whose model called it 18 years out).
- **Freshness window:** 6–12 months; the cycle thesis stays hot through any 2026 housing print.
- **Why this passes the test:** A live rate-shock headline plus a dated, falsifiable cycle that's been right seven times in a row.

## Idea 7 — Why TrumpRx made drugs more expensive

- **News trigger:** 17 pharma companies signed TrumpRx price-cut deals while spending $130M+ lobbying in 2025 and quietly raising list prices on most of the same drugs (01-news.md candidate 14).
- **Evergreen mechanism:** US drug prices aren't set by manufacturing cost — they're set by what PBMs (CVS Caremark, Express Scripts, OptumRx) will pay. Three companies handle ~80% of US prescriptions and collect rebates from manufacturers that don't necessarily reach the patient. Patent thickets (Humira had ~130 patent applications) extend monopolies decades past the original molecule. Headline "price cuts" almost always work by lowering the list price while leaving the net price — what manufacturers actually receive — untouched, because the rebate gets reshuffled instead of removed. The episode opens the PBM black box.
- **Three historical parallels:**
  1. 2003 — Medicare Part D passed with a clause forbidding price negotiation; a literal gift.
  2. 2016 — Mylan EpiPen — list price up 500%; net price up much less; PBMs kept the gap.
  3. 2023 — Humira biosimilars launched; AbbVie's revenue barely dropped because of patent maneuvers.
- **Central villain or beneficiary:** CVS Caremark.
- **Freshness window:** 4–8 weeks; the TrumpRx narrative will keep producing follow-on stories.
- **Why this passes the test:** A current administration headline pretending to lower prices, opened up to expose a 20-year-old pricing machine.

## Idea 8 — California wants 5% from 200 people. They'll get zero.

- **News trigger:** California's 2026 Billionaire Tax Act qualified for the November ballot, proposing a one-time 5% wealth levy on the ~200 residents above $1B (01-news.md candidate 13).
- **Evergreen mechanism:** Wealth taxes almost never collect what they promise because the wealthy don't hold *income* — they hold *assets*, and the code is written around that distinction. Three escape hatches: convert ordinary income into long-term capital gains (taxed at ~20%) via founder shares and carried interest; depreciate real estate against active income (which is why every billionaire owns buildings); pass everything at death with a stepped-up basis, erasing every unrealized gain. ProPublica's 2021 IRS leak documented Bezos, Musk, Buffett, and Bloomberg paying single-digit effective rates while the headline rate was 37%. The episode shows the same three moves a California billionaire will use to make 5% of nothing equal nothing.
- **Three historical parallels:**
  1. 1990 — federal "luxury tax" on yachts — collected almost nothing, killed the US yacht industry, repealed in 3 years.
  2. 2012 — France's 75% millionaire tax — Depardieu and ~42,000 millionaires left; repealed by 2015.
  3. 2017 — TCJA — top rate cut, but effective rates for the Forbes 400 barely moved because they were already near zero.
- **Central villain or beneficiary:** The Forbes 400 (the named beneficiary of the existing code).
- **Freshness window:** 6 months through November ballot.
- **Why this passes the test:** A live ballot initiative that gives us a clean reason to teach the three mechanisms that make every wealth tax theatrical.
